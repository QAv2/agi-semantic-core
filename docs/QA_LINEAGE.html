1

The King's geometry History · 1443–1446

Sejong the Great, fourth king of Joseon (r. 1418–1450), completed the alphabet in the winter of 1443 and promulgated it in 1446 as the Hunminjeongeum (훈민정음, "The Correct Sounds for the Instruction of the People") — 28 letters. The explanatory edition, the Hunminjeongeum Haerye, sets out the design principles; lost for centuries, a copy resurfaced in Andong in 1940, and it is the reason we know the geometry was deliberate, not retrofitted.

"Among the ignorant people, there have been many who, having something to put into words, have in the end been unable to express their feelings. I have been distressed because of this, and have newly designed twenty-eight letters..."
— Sejong's preface

The design is featural — the term is Geoffrey Sampson's (1985), coined for Hangul. The consonants are drawings of the articulators (ㄱ the tongue root blocking the throat, ㄴ the tongue at the ridge, ㅁ the mouth, ㅅ the tooth, ㅇ the open throat), extended by stroke-addition and assigned in the Haerye to the five phases, seasons, and directions. The vowels are three primitives with explicit cosmology, composed into all the rest — with yang and yin classes written into the glyphs (dot above/right vs below/left: vowel harmony as geometry). Letters then compose into square syllable blocks — a two-level algebra a learner can run generatively.

"The wise can learn them before the morning is over; the unwise in ten days... There is no usage not provided for. Even the sound of the winds, the cry of the crane, the cackle of fowl and the barking of dogs — all may be written."
— Jeong Inji's postface

A small atom set plus a composition rule lets a writer spell sounds no one ever wrote before. That sentence is the whole method of this program, five centuries early. The scholarly class protested (Choe Manri, 1444) — the logographic monopoly was a power structure, and a featural alphabet dissolved it. Literacy for the voiceless was the point.

Pulled apart — the 90° rule Discovery

QA's first structural discovery came from taking Jamo composition apart: semantic opposites are complements at ~90°, not antiparallel at 180°. Opposition is a label; the geometry underneath is orthogonal complementarity — two poles sharing a space at right angles, the way ㅡ and ㅣ cross, not the way +x and −x annihilate. This became the immutable encoding rule of the semantic dictionary, and it has held through every validation pass since.

The rule earned an independent echo from a source that knew nothing of the dictionary: Horden's reading of the Penniston sequence — "things in two different times can occupy the same space" — is complement geometry said in narrative. And the embedding studies (Phase 6) showed the other half: statistical training compresses ~95% of complement pairs — the 90° structure is precisely what gradient descent on co-occurrence does not learn. The rule is the dictionary's unique cargo.
